# Case Study 2: the essay that found the unclaimed position

> **What this is.** A complete worked example of a Viral Engine run on written content. It is **not** a verified success story with real traffic numbers. Every metric is a `[bracketed placeholder]`. The method is what's being demonstrated — audit it, disagree with it, use it.

**Input:** *"Blog post about AI anxiety"*

---

## The bet

**What it is.** A post about AI anxiety reframed from *"here's how to feel better about AI"* into *"here is the specific thing you're actually afraid of, and here is what to do about it on Monday."*

**Who it's for.** Mid-career professionals (5–15 years experience) in writing, design, and support roles who use AI tools daily, feel quietly replaceable, and don't say so at work.

**Why it spreads.** AI anxiety content is everywhere, but almost all of it is either hype or reassurance. The unclaimed position is **specific and unsentimental**. Naming the exact fear gives readers language they've been missing — and people share language, not advice.

**Biggest risk.** The topic is saturated. A generic "AI won't take your job" essay gets scrolled past at 200 words. Differentiation has to come from specificity, not from tone.

---

## The diagnosis

| Lever | As given | After repositioning |
|---|---:|---:|
| Surprise | 2 | 4 |
| Identity | 3 | 5 |
| Usefulness | 2 | 4 |
| Emotion | 3 | 4 |
| Cost to share | 3 | 4 |
| Tribal fit | 2 | 4 |
| Remixability | 1 | 3 |

**Bottleneck as given:** surprise + remixability. "AI anxiety" is a category everyone has already written an essay about — nothing about it is unexpected, and there's nothing for a reader to *do* with it afterward.

**Bottleneck after repositioning:** remixability (3). Essays don't fork easily. The fix isn't more emotion — it's a small, adoptable artifact inside the post: a named frame the reader can reuse at work the next morning.

**Verdict.** Publish only if you have a specific, uncomfortable observation. Without one, this is a search-traffic post, not a shareable one — and should be planned as such.

---

## The hooks

### 1. "You're not afraid of AI taking your job. You're afraid of being the last person to notice."

- **Pattern:** reframe
- **Target:** mid-career professionals who suspect something is changing and feel out of the loop
- **Why it works:** contradicts the story the reader has been telling themselves, and replaces it with something more precise and more embarrassing. Recognition is the strongest share trigger in text.
- **How it fails:** requires high emotional accuracy. If readers don't recognize themselves within three lines, the post reads as presumptuous.

### 2. "The uncomfortable truth: your job isn't being automated. It's being unbundled."

- **Pattern:** curiosity gap + enemy
- **Target:** people whose role is a bundle of tasks that AI is cutting apart piece by piece
- **Why it works:** "unbundled" is a specific word most readers haven't applied to themselves. Specific vocabulary earns saves.
- **How it fails:** slightly jargony. Define it in the second sentence or lose the reader.

### 3. "I spent a week tracking what AI actually did to my work. The result wasn't what I expected."

- **Pattern:** receipt + curiosity gap
- **Target:** skeptics who want data, not takes
- **Why it works:** promises evidence and withholds the finding. Also triggers the "just give me the answer" reflex that drives scroll-through.
- **How it fails:** weakest of the three on identity — the reader isn't the subject. Use it as a follow-up post, not the launch.

---

## The positioning

**The crowded category you're leaving:** AI optimism vs. AI doomthink — a binary argued to death that no longer changes minds.

**The sentence:**

> **It's not another AI-takes-jobs essay. It's workplace change literacy — for people whose role is being quietly taken apart while everyone argues about whether AI is overhyped.**

**Why not these:**

| Rejected frame | Why it was cut |
|---|---|
| How to future-proof your career (upskilling) | Honest and useful, but it lands in self-help with zero tension and a saturated hook market. |
| AI is overhyped, here's the evidence | Contrarian takes get shares from one tribe and reflexive dismissal from the other. Splits the audience before it can compound. |

**Proof required.** The frame needs one real, verifiable observation from the author's own work — a task that changed, a timeline, a before/after. Without a first-hand receipt, this collapses into opinion.

---

## The structure

**1. Named pattern.** Name the phenomenon and repeat it at least four times: **"The Unbundling."** Short, sayable, immediately reusable in a meeting or a Slack thread the next morning.
- *Cost to sharer:* nothing — sharing a named idea makes the sharer look articulate.
- *Why:* named concepts outlive posts. If the phrase enters a reader's workplace vocabulary, it distributes itself without further effort.

**2. Template / fork.** End with a short, copyable "unbundling audit" — three questions with blank lines, designed to be pasted into a notes app and filled in over a lunch break.
- *Cost to sharer:* one screenshot or copy-paste.
- *Why:* readers don't share essays. They share things they can forward with "try this on your role" and no further comment.

**Rejected: comment-bait.** No "what do you think?" at the end. It generates low-effort replies that dilute the thread and produce no second wave.

### The content skeleton

> **You're not afraid of AI taking your job. You're afraid of being the last person to notice.**
>
> That fear is rational. But it's pointed at the wrong thing. Your job isn't one object to be replaced — it's twelve tasks held together by habit, and only some of them are moving.
>
> **The proofs:** a specific task from the author's own week with before/after detail (no invented statistics) · the mechanism — unbundling as task-level substitution, not role-level replacement · a counter-example, a role that survived the shift and the structural reason it did, so the reader can locate themselves.
>
> **The turn:** once you see your role as a bundle, the question changes from *"will I be replaced"* to *"which parts of my bundle are still worth a salary"* — and that question has an answer you can act on this week.
>
> **Close:** do the audit. Three questions, one lunch break. If it doesn't change how you explain your job, close the tab.

## Distribution

**Seed nodes** (named, not "social media"):

1. Two newsletters covering work and technology with a skeptical readership — pitch a guest excerpt, not a link
2. One professional community where the target readers actually talk shop (design or support, not general tech)
3. LinkedIn, posted as the thesis only with the audit as the payload — the platform rewards first-person, and this post is first-person
4. A single well-chosen forum thread about role changes, answered properly before linking
5. Five named individuals in the author's network who would forward it — send it to them personally, pre-publication

**The first 100.** Send the draft to 10 people who match the audience and ask one question: *"Which paragraph felt like it was about you?"* Their quoted sentences become the strongest lines in the final edit, and their early shares seed the whole readership.

**The share sentence** — the words a fan uses, not you:

> *"This put words to something I've been feeling for six months and couldn't explain — the unbundling audit is genuinely worth the five minutes."*

**The loop.** Reader runs the audit → describes their own bundle to a colleague → colleague asks what the audit was → the post gets forwarded without the author involved. The named concept is the vehicle.

**The backstop.** A permanently useful reference page ("the unbundling audit") that earns search traffic independent of the launch, plus newsletter capture on that page. The post is the spike; the page is the floor.

---

## First 48 hours

1. **Hour 0–2:** Write only line one and the audit. If those two don't work, the post can't work. Nothing else matters yet.
2. **Hour 2–4:** Confirm the personal observation is real and specific. If it's vague, either get the detail or don't publish this angle.
3. **Hour 4–5:** Send the draft to 10 readers matching the audience. Ask one question, not for approval.
4. **Day 1 evening:** Edit using their quoted lines. Expect to cut 20% of the post.
5. **Day 2 morning:** Publish. Single call to action — the audit. No "let me know your thoughts."
6. **Day 2 afternoon:** Answer the first 20 comments personally. Don't defend the frame; ask what their bundle looks like.
7. **Day 2 evening:** Note which sentence gets quoted back most often. That sentence is the next post.

## Scorecard

| Metric | Working | If it's low |
|---|---|---|
| Saves ÷ views | > 4% | The post is interesting but not usable. Strengthen the audit, weaken the essay. |
| Shares ÷ views | > 1.5% for a text post | No quotable language. The named pattern isn't sticky enough — test a blunter name. |
| Share of first 20 replies describing their own situation | > 50% | You triggered debate instead of recognition. Recognition converts; debate only scrolls. |

---

## The failure condition

> **This fails if the author has no first-hand observation and is writing the frame as commentary.**

Readers can detect borrowed authority in about three paragraphs — and the replies will say so publicly.
