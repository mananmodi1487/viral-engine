# 01 — Why things spread, and why they mostly do not

**20 minutes.** By the end you should be able to explain, without a diagram, why "make it
viral" is not a plan.

## The thing you have probably been told

That ideas travel from person to person, like a disease: one reader tells two, who tell four.
This is the "chain" or "cascade" model. It is the mental picture behind almost every growth
tactic you have read.

## What the evidence actually says

Duncan Watts and colleagues studied this at scale. The finding that matters here, from Goel,
Watts & Goldstein (2012), is uncomfortable: **most of the diffusion people attribute to
person-to-person spreading is better explained by a single broadcast.**

Someone with an audience posts it once. Some fraction of their audience looks. Very little
further passing-on happens. We perceive a cascade because we see the original post and then we
see people talking about it, and we assume the second group got it from the first group rather
than from the same post.

Two consequences:

1. **Optimising for passes is optimising for something rare.** You would be building for the
   uncommon path.
2. **The single broadcast is where leverage lives.** One specific person deciding to post it is
   worth more than a hundred people "liking" it quietly.

There is a second finding with a similar shape. Berger & Milkman (2012) showed emotional
activation predicts sharing: content that produces awe, anger, or anxiety travels further than
content that produces sadness or contentment. This is real, and it is also the most dangerous
finding in this field, because the easiest way to produce anger is to lie about who to be
angry at. `evidence/ethics-incidents/` exists partly because of this.

Third, from Berger's *Contagious*: only about 7% of word of mouth happens online. Most of what
makes something travel is invisible to every dashboard you will ever use.

## What this means for you

Your plan is not "get it to spread". Your plan is one or more of:

- Get **one specific person** with an audience to post it, for a reason that is about them and
  not about you.
- Make the thing **forwardable by a non-expert** in ten seconds, because the person who needs
  it is usually not the person who found it.
- Make it **worth describing** by someone who has no stake in it.

All three are engineering problems about clarity, not about reach.

## Exercise

`../exercises/01-broadcast-not-chain.md`

Take a thing you made. Write down the name of **one specific person or one specific role** who
could post it. Not "the community". A role: "the person who runs the internal engineering
newsletter at a 200-person company". Then write why they would — in terms of what it does for
their readers, not for you.

If you cannot, that is the finding. It is more useful than a plan.

## What this module cannot tell you

- It cannot tell you the base rate of success, because nobody honestly knows it, and anyone
  quoting one is guessing.
- It does not claim the chain model never happens. Occasionally something really does cascade.
  The claim is that you cannot plan for the exception, and that planning as if it were the rule
  is why most launch advice fails.
- Everything above is other people's published research, summarised by one author who may have
  oversimplified it. Read the papers.
